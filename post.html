What Meta Ads taught me about human attention

Mohammed Tahaan Ziyad January 2026 5 min read

When I started running Meta Ads for clients at The Growth Waves, I thought the job was mostly technical. Set the targeting, write some copy, hit publish, read the numbers. I was wrong about almost everything.

What running real campaigns — with real budgets and real clients waiting for results — actually teaches you is something no course covers well: people are not rational. They scroll, they glance, they feel, they move on. You have maybe one second to exist for them.

The scroll is the ultimate test

A Meta feed is one of the most competitive attention environments ever created. Every single post, ad, reel, and story is fighting for the same finite resource: a human being's willingness to pause. Most ads fail this test not because they're badly designed, but because they don't answer the one question every scrolling thumb is asking — "is this worth stopping for?"

"Attention is not given. It's earned in the first frame."

The first image or video frame is everything. I started thinking about it less as an ad and more as a question being asked of the viewer. What do they see in 0.5 seconds? Does it feel familiar enough to register, and different enough to interrupt?

Data is just behaviour made visible

Every click, every skip, every 3-second video view is someone making a decision. The metrics are just a record of those decisions at scale. When I started reading the numbers this way — as a trail of human choices rather than a performance report — something clicked.

A low CTR on an ad isn't a design problem. It's a relevance problem. The right people aren't seeing it, or the right people are seeing it but don't believe the promise. Both are fixable, but only if you understand what question you're actually answering.

Three months at TGW didn't make me an expert. But it gave me something more useful than expertise at this stage: a working intuition about how attention actually behaves in the wild, built from watching real people make real choices in real time.

That's the kind of thing textbooks can describe but can't give you. You have to earn it one campaign at a time.
